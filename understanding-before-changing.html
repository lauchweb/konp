Understanding Before Changing

There is often an expectation that good work begins with an answer. Something needs to improve, so something must change. An experience feels difficult, so it must be redesigned. A product is not performing as expected, so something must be added, removed, or replaced.

But change is not necessarily progress.

Before deciding what should be different, it is worth understanding what is already there.

Not only what something does, but why it has become what it is. Who uses it. When they use it. What they expect from it. What surrounds it. What has accumulated around it over time. What is deliberate, and what is simply the result of decisions made before.

Looking closely can reveal that what first appears to be a problem is sometimes only a symptom.

A complicated interface may not have an interface problem. A confusing service may not need another layer of communication. A product that feels difficult may be carrying decisions that were made elsewhere, for reasons that are no longer visible.

Without understanding the whole, it is easy to improve the wrong thing.

There is something reassuring about making a change. A new feature is visible. A redesigned interface looks different. A new process can be described, measured, and presented.

Understanding is less visible.

It takes time to observe. To ask questions. To follow something through its different contexts. To notice the small inconsistencies that initially seem unimportant. To understand what people actually do rather than what they are expected to do.

It can feel as though nothing is happening.

But this is often where the most important decisions are being made.

The decision not to change something can be as considered as the decision to change it.

Nothing exists entirely on its own. A product sits within an experience. An experience sits within a context. A context is shaped by people, expectations, habits, environments, and systems.

Changing one part can affect all the others.

This is why an isolated improvement can sometimes make the overall experience worse. A feature may work perfectly on its own while making the wider product more difficult. An object can be carefully considered yet feel disconnected from the way it is presented or used. A service can become efficient internally while remaining frustrating to the person experiencing it.

The individual part may be correct.

The whole may not be.

Understanding means moving between these levels without losing sight of either.

Once something is understood more clearly, another question becomes possible: what actually needs to be there?

Not everything that exists needs to remain.

Some things have accumulated because they were useful once. Some remain because nobody questioned them. Some are there because adding something felt easier than removing it.

Simplicity is therefore not a visual quality alone. It is not a matter of making something look minimal. It comes from knowing what is necessary and having the confidence to leave the rest alone.

That requires judgement.

A detail may be small but essential. Another may be prominent but unnecessary. One element may carry meaning, while another merely creates noise.

The work is not to remove as much as possible.

It is to understand enough to know what deserves to remain.

Good work does not always begin by asking what should be made. Sometimes it begins with a quieter question:

What is this really trying to be?

From there, other things become clearer. What is its purpose? What should it feel like? What should it make possible? What belongs to it? What has been added without reason? What has been overlooked?

Only after those questions have been explored does change become meaningful.

The objective is not to change for the sake of improvement. It is to understand deeply enough that what should happen next becomes clearer.

Sometimes that means making something different.

Sometimes it means making less.

And sometimes the most considered decision is to leave something exactly as it is.
